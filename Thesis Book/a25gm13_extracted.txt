7. Introduction
The

Digital
magnitude
comparators are fundamen-tal building blocks in modern digital systems because they
determine the relative relationship between two binary inputs. A 1-bit magnitude comparator
produces three possible outputs in-dicating whether input A is greater than input B, equal to
input B, or less than input B. Such circuits are widely used in arithmetic logic units (ALUs),
processors, control systems, digital signal processing units, memory systems, and other VLSI
applications.
The implementation of a comparator at the transistor level is strongly influenced by the
selected logic style. Static CMOS logic provides reliable operation, good noise margins, and
full voltage swing, but it may require a relatively large number of transistors. Transmission
Gate (TG) logic can reduce transistor count and provide efficient signal transmission. Pass
Transistor Logic (PTL) offers compact circuit implementation and potentially lower power
consumption,

Diffusion Input (GDI) logic provides another low-complexity implementation technique that
can reduce transistor count, power consumption, and circuit area.
Therefore, selecting an appropriate logic style requires a systematic comparison of
impor-tant performance parameters rather than considering a single parameter. This research
proposes the design and performance comparison of a 1-bit magnitude comparator
implemented using Static CMOS, Transmission Gate (TG), Pass Transistor Logic (PTL), and
Gate Diffusion In-put (GDI) logic styles. The designs will be implemented at the transistor
level and evaluated using simulation. The comparison will focus on propagation delay,
average power consump-tion, transistor count, and Power Delay Product (PDP). The study is
expected to identify the most suitable logic style for efficient 1-bit magnitude comparator
design for low-power and high-performance VLSI applications.
8. Literature Review
CMOS logic design has been extensively investigated to achieve lower power consumption,
higher operating speed, reduced transistor count, and improved area efficiency. Conventional
Static CMOS remains one of the most widely used logic implementation techniques because
of
its
robust
operation,
full
voltage
swing,
and
good
noise immunity. However,
complementary pull-up and pull-down networks can result in a comparatively higher
transistor count for certain logic functions.
Alternative logic styles have been introduced to overcome some of the limitations of
con-ventional CMOS implementation. Transmission Gate (TG) logic uses parallel NMOS
and PMOS devices to transmit both logic levels effectively. This approach can provide
efficient switching and reduce circuit complexity for selected digital functions. However, the
use of additional control signals and transistor sizing can influence overall circuit
performance.
Pass Transistor Logic (PTL) reduces the number of transistors by using transistors as
con-trolled switches. PTL-based circuits can achieve compact implementation and lower
switching activity. However, threshold-voltage degradation, reduced voltage swing, and
signal restoration requirements may affect the reliability and speed of the circuit.
Gate Diffusion Input (GDI) is another low-power logic design technique that modifies the
conventional CMOS structure by applying different input signals to the gate and diffusion
ter-minals of MOS transistors. GDI can significantly reduce transistor count and switching
activity for several logic functions. Nevertheless, its performance can depend on the
technology, tran-sistor sizing, input configuration, and output restoration requirements.
Magnitude comparators have been implemented using different CMOS logic techniques
to achieve a balance among speed, power, area, and circuit complexity. Previous research
generally
emphasizes
one
or
two
performance parameters, while a comprehensive
comparison of Static CMOS, TG, PTL, and GDI implementations of the same 1-bit
magnitude comparator under identical simulation conditions is less frequently emphasized.
This research therefore focuses on implementing the same 1-bit magnitude comparator us-

con-ditions. The comparison will provide a clearer understanding of the trade-offs among

9. Problem Statement / Research Gap
Although several logic styles are available for implementing digital comparator circuits, each
technique has different advantages and limitations. Static CMOS generally provides reliable
full-swing operation and good noise margins, but its complementary transistor structure can
in-crease transistor count and switching capacitance. TG logic can provide efficient signal
trans-mission but may introduce additional control complexity. PTL can reduce transistor
count and circuit complexity, but threshold-voltage degradation may affect the output signal
level. GDI can achieve highly compact implementations and low switching activity, but its
performance and voltage-level behavior may vary depending on circuit topology and
operating conditions.
Furthermore, comparing different logic styles using different circuit structures, simulation
conditions, or performance criteria can make it difficult to determine which technique
provides the best overall implementation for a 1-bit magnitude comparator. A direct and
systematic comparison of Static CMOS, TG, PTL, and GDI logic styles using the same
comparator func-tionality and consistent evaluation criteria is therefore required.
The research gap addressed in this work is the lack of a unified performance comparison
of these four logic styles for a 1-bit magnitude comparator under equivalent design and
simulation conditions. The study will investigate the trade-offs among propagation delay,
average power consumption, transistor count, and Power Delay Product. The findings will
help identify the logic style that provides the most suitable balance between speed, power
efficiency, and circuit complexity.
10. Objectives with Specific Aims
General Objective
To design and perform a comparative analysis of a 1-bit magnitude comparator using Static
CMOS, Transmission Gate (TG), Pass Transistor Logic (PTL), and Gate Diffusion Input
(GDI) logic styles.
Specific Objectives
1. To design transistor-level 1-bit magnitude comparator circuits using Static CMOS,
TG, PTL, and GDI logic styles.
2. To simulate and verify the functional operation of all four comparator
implementa-tions under identical simulation conditions.

3. To measure and analyze propagation delay, average power consumption, transistor
count, and Power Delay Product (PDP) for each logic style.
4. To compare the performance of Static CMOS, TG, PTL, and GDI implementations
and identify the most suitable logic style based on the obtained performance
trade-offs.
11. Proposed Methodology
The proposed research will follow a systematic design, simulation, verification, and
perfor-mance evaluation procedure. First, relevant literature on 1-bit magnitude comparators
and CMOS logic styles will be reviewed to identify existing design approaches and research
gaps. Based on the literature review, the required design specifications and performance
parameters will be established.
A 1-bit magnitude comparator will then be designed using four different logic styles:
Static CMOS, Transmission Gate (TG), Pass Transistor Logic (PTL), and Gate Diffusion
Input (GDI). The same Boolean functionality and equivalent input conditions will be
maintained for all four implementations to ensure a fair comparison.
The transistor-level circuits will be implemented and simulated using an appropriate
SPICE-based simulation environment. Functional verification will be performed for all
possible com-binations of the two binary inputs. After successful functional verification, the
performance of each implementation will be evaluated using propagation delay, average
power consumption, transistor count, and Power Delay Product (PDP).
Finally, the obtained results will be organized and compared using suitable tables and
graphs. The comparative analysis will identify the strengths and limitations of each logic
style and determine which implementation provides the most appropriate balance between
speed, power consumption, and circuit complexity.

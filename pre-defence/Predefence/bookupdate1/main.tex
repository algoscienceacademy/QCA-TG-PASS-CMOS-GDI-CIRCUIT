\chapter*{ABSTRACT}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{ABSTRACT}

\noindent The rapid expansion of mobile computing, wearable electronics, implantable biomedical devices, wireless sensor networks, and edge computing architectures has elevated power minimization to a paramount design objective in modern Very Large Scale Integration (VLSI) systems. Among the foundational building blocks of digital computing architectures, digital magnitude comparators play an indispensable role in arithmetic logic units (ALUs), central processing units (CPUs), digital signal processors (DSPs), data sorting engines, and high-speed flash analog-to-digital converters (ADCs). Conventional digital comparators implemented using standard static Complementary Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor (CMOS) logic provide robust noise margins, stable full-rail output voltage swing, and reliable switching characteristics; however, they require a high transistor count (typically 32 transistors for a 1-bit magnitude comparator), resulting in elevated active and leakage power dissipation, large parasitic interconnect capacitance, and increased silicon footprint.

\noindent To overcome these intrinsic limitations, this thesis presents the comprehensive design, transistor-level implementation, and multi-parameter performance analysis of an ultra-low-power, high-speed 1-bit magnitude comparator based on the Gate Diffusion Input (GDI) technique. The GDI methodology represents an effective low-power circuit design paradigm that permits the realization of complex logic functions with a substantially reduced transistor count compared to conventional CMOS and pass-transistor implementations. The proposed GDI-based 1-bit comparator circuit accomplishes the generation of all three arithmetic comparison outputs ($A>B$, $A=B$, and $A<B$) using only 10 transistors (5 NMOS and 5 PMOS), achieving a remarkable 68.75\% reduction in transistor count over the standard 32-transistor static CMOS design.

\noindent The proposed circuit, along with conventional static CMOS, Transmission Gate Logic (TGL), and Pass Transistor Logic (PTL) comparator benchmarks, has been modeled and rigorously evaluated using LTSpice with the Predictive Technology Model (PTM) at the 180nm CMOS technology node under a supply voltage of 1.8V. The simulation results confirm complete functional correctness across all four possible input combinations. Extensive quantitative evaluations reveal that the proposed GDI comparator achieves an exceptionally low average power dissipation of 34.18~pW, representing a 73.83\% power reduction compared to static CMOS (130.61~pW) and a 10.34\% reduction compared to PTL (38.12~pW). Furthermore, the proposed design achieves a minimum propagation delay of 336~ps for the equality ($A=B$) output, outperforming static CMOS (398.09~ps), transmission gate (587.24~ps), and pass transistor (421.77~ps) designs by 15.59\%, 42.78\%, and 20.33\%, respectively. The resulting Power-Delay Product (PDP) of 0.0000114~fJ confirms that the proposed GDI comparator achieves the highest energy efficiency per switching operation among all evaluated configurations. Extended multi-node scaling investigations across 180nm, 90nm, and 45nm technology regimes demonstrate sustained performance scalability. This research delivers an area-efficient, low-power digital comparator suitable for next-generation energy-constrained VLSI applications.

\chapter{INTRODUCTION}

\section{Background and Technological Context}

The continuous advancement of microelectronics has reshaped modern society, underpinning ubiquitous digital computation, wireless communication networks, biomedical instrumentation, and artificial intelligence systems. Over the past five decades, exponential integration density and geometric device scaling have transformed room-sized computing machinery into compact, battery-powered portable platforms and autonomous edge devices. Today, digital integrated circuits process trillions of operations per second, operating across environments ranging from high-performance cloud data centers to energy-harvesting Internet-of-Things (IoT) sensor nodes and implantable biomedical monitoring systems.

As electronic systems permeate mission-critical and energy-constrained domains, power dissipation has surpassed raw clock frequency as the foremost figure of merit in Very Large Scale Integration (VLSI) design. In portable electronics, power dissipation directly dictates battery life and physical form factor. In implantable medical technologies, such as cardiac pacemakers and deep-brain neurostimulators, energy consumption governs device longevity, where battery exhaustion mandates high-risk surgical intervention. Concurrently, in hyperscale computing installations, severe localized thermal dissipation (hotspots) escalates cooling expenditures and accelerates physical device wear-out. Consequently, contemporary circuit designers face the demanding requirement to achieve sub-nanosecond operational latencies while strictly constraining power consumption within the picowatt and nanowatt domains.

At the core of digital computing architectures lies a finite collection of fundamental combinational and arithmetic building blocks, including adders, multiplexers, decoders, and digital magnitude comparators. Among these primitives, binary magnitude comparators are among the most frequently executed arithmetic units in modern processors. These circuits perform relational decision-making, conditional branching, address tag matching, thermometer code decoding, and data sorting. Given their pervasive instantiation across wide execution datapaths, any architectural or topological enhancement that curtails power consumption, switching delay, or silicon area in an elementary comparator cell yields compounding efficiency benefits across the entire VLSI system.

\section{Semiconductor Evolution and the Low-Power Paradigm}

The historical trajectory of semiconductor scaling was guided for decades by Gordon Moore's empirical observation in 1965, widely known as Moore's Law, which stated that the density of transistors integrated on a monolithic silicon chip doubles roughly every two years [1]. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:moores_law}, physical gate geometries scaled from tens of micrometers down to deep sub-micron and nanometer dimensions, enabling the integration of billions of Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistors (MOSFETs) on a single die.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{moores_law_scaling.jpg}
\caption{Historical trajectory of transistor density scaling and minimum feature size reduction in semiconductor technology from 1970 to 2025.}
\label{fig:moores_law}
\end{figure}

Throughout the 1970s and 1980s, dimensional scaling was governed by Robert Dennard's constant electric-field scaling theory [2]. Dennard demonstrated that by scaling physical transistor dimensions (channel length $L$, channel width $W$, and oxide thickness $t_{ox}$) along with supply voltage ($V_{DD}$) and threshold voltage ($V_{th}$) by a common scaling factor $\kappa > 1$, circuits achieved higher operating frequencies while maintaining a constant power density per unit silicon area.

However, as channel lengths shrank below the 180nm threshold, classical Dennard scaling encountered fundamental physical limits. Due to the invariant Boltzmann thermodynamic distribution of carrier energies at room temperature ($300\text{ K}$), the subthreshold swing of bulk silicon MOSFETs cannot scale below the theoretical limit of approximately $60\text{ mV/decade}$. As a result, further lowering of the supply voltage without a corresponding reduction in threshold voltage degraded switching speeds; conversely, scaling $V_{th}$ downward resulted in exponential increases in subthreshold leakage currents. This breakdown gave rise to the contemporary ``Power Wall'' and the phenomenon of ``Dark Silicon,'' in which thermal dissipation constraints prevent all transistors on a chip from operating simultaneously at peak clock rates [3, 12]. Consequently, power minimization has transitioned from a secondary optimization parameter into the primary design bottleneck governing modern digital system design.

\section{Role of Transistor-Level Circuit Design in VLSI Hierarchy}

To navigate these scaling barriers, modern integrated circuit design follows a hierarchical, multi-stage abstraction flow spanning system architecture to silicon fabrication, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:vlsi_flow}.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{vlsi_design_flow.jpg}
\caption{Hierarchical VLSI design flow showing the progression from high-level specifications down to physical layout and fabrication, highlighting the central role of transistor-level circuit design.}
\label{fig:vlsi_flow}
\end{figure}

While architectural optimizations (such as pipelining, multi-core parallelism, and dynamic voltage and frequency scaling) provide substantial energy savings, the fundamental lower bound on power and silicon area is ultimately dictated by the transistor-level circuit design stage. At this level, decisions regarding the underlying logic style (e.g., Static CMOS, Pass Transistor Logic, Transmission Gate Logic, or Gate Diffusion Input), transistor counts, aspect ratios ($W/L$), and parasitic loading establish the intrinsic energy efficiency of the standard cell library. An architectural block instantiated thousands of times cannot outperform the fundamental physical limits of its constituent elementary cells. Thus, optimizing fundamental arithmetic primitives at the transistor level provides a high-leverage opportunity to enhance overall system performance.

\section{Digital Magnitude Comparators in Modern Computing Systems}

A digital magnitude comparator is a combinational logic circuit designed to evaluate two binary words and establish their relative arithmetic relationship [1]. Figure~\ref{fig:comparator_block} depicts the functional block representation of a 1-bit magnitude comparator.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{comparator_block_diagram.jpg}
\caption{Functional block diagram of a 1-bit binary magnitude comparator receiving single-bit inputs ($A, B$) and generating arithmetic status flags ($A>B$, $A=B$, $A<B$).}
\label{fig:comparator_block}
\end{figure}

The 1-bit comparator takes two single-bit inputs, $A$ and $B$, and computes three mutually exclusive arithmetic output conditions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Greater-Than Output ($A>B$):} Asserted high when input $A$ exceeds input $B$ ($A=1, B=0$).
\item \textbf{Equality Output ($A=B$):} Asserted high when input $A$ is identical to input $B$ ($A=0, B=0$ or $A=1, B=1$).
\item \textbf{Less-Than Output ($A<B$):} Asserted high when input $A$ is smaller than input $B$ ($A=0, B=1$).
\end{enumerate}

Although the 1-bit comparator operates on single-bit inputs, it serves as the essential foundational cell for wider data-word comparators (e.g., 8-bit, 16-bit, 32-bit, and 64-bit architectures) via iterative ripple-carry cascading or tree structures. Digital magnitude comparators perform vital operational tasks across modern computing systems:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Arithmetic Logic Units and Processor Pipelines:} Comparators execute conditional branch determinations, loop termination evaluations, zero detection, and register tag dependency checks in out-of-order superscalar execution engines [3].
\item \textbf{Ultra-High-Speed Flash ADCs:} In flash analog-to-digital converters, dense arrays of digital comparators decode the thermometer codes generated by analog front-end preamplifiers into binary representations at multi-gigahertz sampling rates. In this context, comparator power dissipation and latency directly define the Effective Number of Bits (ENOB) and power efficiency of the data converter [1].
\item \textbf{Cache Tag and Translation Lookaside Buffer Matching:} In high-performance microprocessor memory hierarchies, wide comparators perform rapid address-tag matching to evaluate cache hits and misses, where comparison propagation delay sits directly in the critical access path [4].
\item \textbf{Hardware Data Sorting Engines:} In database accelerators and network packet filtering switches, comparators form the fundamental processing nodes of bitonic and odd-even sorting networks operating at line speed [6].
\item \textbf{Implantable Biomedical and Edge IoT Sensors:} In pacemaker event detection and remote environmental sensor nodes, comparators continuously evaluate incoming biosignals against reference safety thresholds, triggering wake-up events from deep sleep modes [3].
\end{itemize}

\section{Research Motivation and Key Drivers}

The wide deployment of digital comparators makes them a prime candidate for aggressive circuit-level optimization. In traditional digital libraries, standard static Complementary Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor (CMOS) logic has long been the default implementation style due to its robust noise margins, full rail-to-rail voltage swings, and high noise immunity. However, static CMOS requires complementary pairs of pull-up (PMOS) and pull-down (NMOS) networks, leading to a high transistor count: a conventional 1-bit magnitude comparator requires 32 transistors [1]. This large device count introduces substantial internal node capacitances, elevated dynamic switching energy ($C_{load} V_{DD}^2 f$), multi-path subthreshold leakage, and a large silicon die footprint.

In response, alternative digital design paradigms have been proposed. Pass Transistor Logic (PTL) dramatically reduces transistor count to 10 transistors by utilizing NMOS pass transistors. However, PTL experiences an inherent threshold voltage drop ($V_{DD} - V_{thn}$), which severely compromises noise margins and causes static current leakage in subsequent logic stages [2]. Transmission Gate Logic (TGL) restores full rail-to-rail signal levels through complementary parallel NMOS/PMOS pairs, but requires complementary control signals that necessitate inverter stages, increasing the device count to 16--24 transistors and increasing dynamic routing power [3]. Recent hybrid topologies combine multiple logic families but frequently require 24 or more transistors and consume microwatt-level power, which is excessive for battery-less, energy-harvesting applications [3, 4].

The Gate Diffusion Input (GDI) technique, introduced by Morgenshtein, Fish, and Wagner [5], presents an attractive alternative for low-power VLSI design. By using a twin-transistor cell with independently driven gate and diffusion terminals, complex logic operations—such as 2:1 multiplexers, AND gates, and XNOR functions—can be realized using only two transistors, compared to 6 to 12 transistors in conventional static CMOS. A complete 1-bit magnitude comparator producing all three arithmetic flags can thus be implemented with only 10 transistors. This substantial reduction in device count presents an opportunity to achieve significant reductions in parasitic capacitance, dynamic power, and physical layout area, making the GDI technique an attractive approach for ultra-low-power digital arithmetic circuits.

\section{Problem Statement}

Digital magnitude comparators implemented in deep-submicron CMOS technologies face challenging and conflicting design requirements: they must achieve ultra-low average power consumption to support energy-constrained operating environments, deliver sub-nanosecond propagation latencies to avoid stalling high-speed processing pipelines, and occupy minimal silicon die area, all while preserving output signal integrity and logic functionality across all input combinations.

Conventional 32-transistor static CMOS comparators suffer from excessive parasitic loading and elevated active power dissipation due to redundant device counts [1]. Pass Transistor Logic reduces transistor count but suffers from threshold voltage degradation that threatens reliable signal transmission [2]. Transmission Gate Logic restores signal swings but incurs high routing complexity, larger device counts (16--24T), and higher dynamic switching currents [3]. Furthermore, previously reported leakage-reduction techniques, such as transistor stacking and Multi-Threshold CMOS (MTCMOS), incur severe delay penalties (exceeding tens of nanoseconds) or demand costly non-standard fabrication masks [1, 2].

Consequently, there is an unmet engineering need for a minimal-transistor, ultra-low-power 1-bit magnitude comparator that achieves picowatt-level power dissipation, maintains sub-nanosecond switching performance, operates within a standard single-threshold bulk CMOS process, and avoids output degradation. This thesis addresses this design challenge through the systematic development and benchmarking of a 10-transistor Gate Diffusion Input (GDI) magnitude comparator.

\section{Research Objectives and Specific Aims}

The primary objective of this thesis is to design, model, analyze, and benchmark an ultra-low-power 10-transistor 1-bit magnitude comparator based on the Gate Diffusion Input technique, and to comprehensively evaluate its performance against conventional and state-of-the-art comparator topologies.

To accomplish this primary objective, the following specific research aims are established:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Mathematical Formulation:} To derive the formal Boolean equations, truth tables, and switching conditions for all three comparator outputs ($A>B$, $A=B$, and $A<B$), establishing the theoretical basis for minimal-transistor mapping.
\item \textbf{Circuit Synthesis via GDI:} To synthesize an optimized 1-bit comparator circuit utilizing the GDI technique, realizing all three comparison functions alongside complementary input generation with a minimal footprint of exactly 10 transistors (5 PMOS and 5 NMOS).
\item \textbf{Benchmark Topology Implementation:} To design, size, and model three reference comparator topologies—a 32-transistor static CMOS comparator, a 10-transistor pass transistor logic (PTL) comparator, and a 16-transistor transmission gate logic (TGL) comparator—under identical electrical constraints.
\item \textbf{Transistor-Level SPICE Simulation:} To execute high-precision transient SPICE simulations in LTSpice utilizing the Berkeley Predictive Technology Model (PTM) 180nm bulk CMOS model deck at a nominal 1.8V supply, confirming functional correctness across all input vectors.

\section{Theoretical Foundations and Arithmetic Architecture of Comparators}

A binary magnitude comparator is a combinational digital subsystem designed to compare the numerical values of two binary representations and establish their arithmetic relationship. In its canonical single-bit form, the comparator receives two 1-bit binary operands, designated as $A$ and $B$, and evaluates three mutually exclusive relational flags:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Greater-Than Condition ($A>B$):} The output evaluates to logic high ($1$) if and only if $A=1$ and $B=0$.
\item \textbf{Equality Condition ($A=B$):} The output evaluates to logic high ($1$) if and only if both bits are arithmetically identical, i.e., $A=B=0$ or $A=B=1$.
\item \textbf{Less-Than Condition ($A<B$):} The output evaluates to logic high ($1$) if and only if $A=0$ and $B=1$.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Input A} & \textbf{Input B} & \textbf{Output ($A>B$)} & \textbf{Output ($A=B$)} & \textbf{Output ($A<B$)} \\

\label{eq:lit_aeb}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
(A < B) = \overline{A} \cdot B
\label{eq:lit_alb}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Architectural Cascading for Multi-Bit Comparison}

In wide datapath processor architectures, $n$-bit words $A = (A_{n-1} A_{n-2} \dots A_0)$ and $B = (B_{n-1} B_{n-2} \dots B_0)$ are compared by cascading single-bit comparator stages. There are two primary architectural topologies:

\noindent\textbf{1. Ripple-Carry Comparison Architecture:}\\
In iterative ripple cascading, comparison begins at the Most Significant Bit (MSB, $k=n-1$) and ripples downward to the Least Significant Bit (LSB, $k=0$). Global word equivalence requires unanimous bit-pair equivalence:
\begin{equation}
(A = B)_n = \prod_{k=0}^{n-1} (A_k \odot B_k)
\end{equation}
The global greater-than decision is resolved hierarchically by determining the highest-order bit position where $A_k \neq B_k$:
\begin{equation}
(A > B)_n = (A_{n-1} \overline{B_{n-1}}) + \sum_{k=0}^{n-2} \left[ \left(\prod_{j=k+1}^{n-1} (A_j \odot B_j)\right) (A_k \overline{B_k}) \right]
\end{equation}
Similarly, the global less-than flag evaluates as:
\begin{equation}
(A < B)_n = (\overline{A_{n-1}} B_{n-1}) + \sum_{k=0}^{n-2} \left[ \left(\prod_{j=k+1}^{n-1} (A_j \odot B_j)\right) (\overline{A_k} B_k) \right]
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{2. Parallel-Prefix and Tree Architectures:}\\
For wide words (32-bit or 64-bit), tree-structured comparators compute local generate ($G_k = A_k \overline{B_k}$), propagate ($P_k = A_k \odot B_k$), and kill ($K_k = \overline{A_k} B_k$) status flags, combining them across logarithmic prefix networks ($\mathcal{O}(\log_2 n)$ latency).

In both configurations, the elementary 1-bit comparator cell is replicated dozens of times. Consequently, any savings in transistor count, capacitive loading, or leakage power achieved in the elementary cell translates to substantial area and power reductions across the entire execution datapath.

\subsection{MOSFET Conduction Regimes}

In an NMOS device with channel width $W$, gate length $L$, gate oxide thickness $t_{ox}$, and oxide capacitance per unit area $C_{ox} = \epsilon_{ox}/t_{ox}$, the drain current $I_{ds}$ spans three operational regimes:

\noindent\textbf{1. Cutoff (Subthreshold) Regime ($V_{gs} < V_{th}$):}\\
The surface potential is below strong inversion. Carrier transport occurs primarily via diffusion across the source-channel barrier rather than drift, resulting in exponential subthreshold leakage.

\noindent\textbf{2. Linear (Triode) Regime ($V_{gs} > V_{th}$ and $V_{ds} < V_{gs} - V_{th}$):}\\
An inverted continuous channel links source and drain. Operating as a voltage-controlled resistor, the drain current is governed by:
\begin{equation}
I_{ds} = \mu_n C_{ox} \frac{W}{L} \left[(V_{gs} - V_{th})V_{ds} - \frac{V_{ds}^2}{2}\right]
\label{eq:ids_lin}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{3. Saturation Regime ($V_{gs} > V_{th}$ and $V_{ds} \ge V_{gs} - V_{th}$):}\\
The inversion layer pinches off near the drain junction. The saturation current, modified by channel-length modulation parameter $\lambda$, is modeled as:
\begin{equation}
I_{ds} = \frac{1}{2} \mu_n C_{ox} \frac{W}{L} (V_{gs} - V_{th})^2 (1 + \lambda V_{ds})
\label{eq:ids_sat_ch2}
\end{equation}
In PMOS devices, conduction dynamics are governed by analogous relationships with reversed polarities and hole mobility $\mu_p$ replacing electron mobility $\mu_n$. In bulk silicon, electron mobility is significantly higher than hole mobility ($\mu_n \approx 500\text{ cm}^2/\text{V}\cdot\text{s}$ vs. $\mu_p \approx 200\text{ cm}^2/\text{V}\cdot\text{s}$), creating an intrinsic drive strength disparity of approximately $2.0$ to $2.5$.

\subsection{Threshold Voltage and Body-Effect Modulation}

The zero-bias threshold voltage $V_{th0}$ represents the gate bias necessary to establish strong surface inversion ($2\phi_F$ surface band bending):
\begin{equation}
V_{th0} = V_{FB} + 2\phi_F + \frac{\sqrt{2 q \epsilon_{si} N_A (2\phi_F)}}{C_{ox}}
\label{eq:vth0_ch2}
\end{equation}
where $V_{FB}$ is flatband potential, $\phi_F = (kT/q)\ln(N_A/n_i)$ is Fermi potential, $\epsilon_{si}$ is silicon permittivity, and $N_A$ is substrate acceptor doping.

When the transistor source potential rises above the bulk substrate potential ($V_{sb} > 0$), the depletion charge under the gate expands. This body effect increases the effective threshold voltage according to:
\begin{equation}
V_{th} = V_{th0} + \gamma \left(\sqrt{2\phi_F + V_{sb}} - \sqrt{2\phi_F}\right)
\label{eq:bodyeffect_ch2}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma = \sqrt{2 q \epsilon_{si} N_A}/C_{ox}$ is the body-effect coefficient. In Pass Transistor Logic (PTL) and Gate Diffusion Input (GDI) topologies, source terminals are driven dynamically rather than being tied permanently to supply or ground rails. Consequently, non-zero $V_{sb}$ induces threshold shifts that must be carefully accounted for during circuit sizing and simulation.

\subsection{CMOS Inverter Characteristics and Voltage Transfer Characteristics (VTC)}

The CMOS inverter consists of a complementary PMOS pull-up and NMOS pull-down pair, serving as the foundational reference cell for digital logic. Figure~\ref{fig:cmos_inverter} illustrates the inverter schematic and its Voltage Transfer Characteristic (VTC).

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{cmos_inverter_vtc.jpg}
\caption{CMOS inverter: (left) schematic showing PMOS pull-up and NMOS pull-down; (right) Voltage Transfer Characteristic (VTC) curve defining noise margins $NM_H$, $NM_L$, and switching threshold $V_M$.}
\label{fig:cmos_inverter}
\end{figure}

The VTC curve defines key digital robustness parameters:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Output High Voltage ($V_{OH}$):} Equal to $V_{DD}$, providing full rail-to-rail swing.
\item \textbf{Output Low Voltage ($V_{OL}$):} Equal to $0\text{V}$ (ground).
\item \textbf{Noise Margin High ($NM_H$):} $NM_H = V_{OH} - V_{IH}$.
\item \textbf{Noise Margin Low ($NM_L$):} $NM_L = V_{IL} - V_{OL}$.
\item \textbf{Switching Threshold ($V_M$):} The input potential where $V_{out} = V_{in}$, derived as:
\begin{equation}
V_M = \frac{V_{DD} + V_{thp} + V_{thn}\sqrt{\beta_n/\beta_p}}{1 + \sqrt{\beta_n/\beta_p}}
\label{eq:vm_def}
\end{equation}
\end{itemize}
To obtain symmetric switching ($V_M = V_{DD}/2$), transconductance parameters must be matched ($\beta_n \approx \beta_p$), requiring the PMOS device to be sized wider than the NMOS device ($W_p/W_n \approx \mu_n/\mu_p \approx 2.0$ to $2.5$).

\subsection{Physical Mechanisms of Power Dissipation}

Total power dissipation in digital CMOS VLSI circuits consists of three distinct physical components [6]:
\begin{equation}
P_{total} = P_{dyn} + P_{sc} + P_{stat}
\label{eq:ptot_decomp}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{1. Dynamic Switching Power ($P_{dyn}$):}\\
Dynamic dissipation occurs when node capacitances are charged to $V_{DD}$ and discharged to ground during logical transitions:
\begin{equation}
P_{dyn} = \alpha_{0 \rightarrow 1} \cdot C_{load} \cdot V_{DD}^2 \cdot f
\label{eq:pdyn_form}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_{0 \rightarrow 1}$ represents the transition activity factor, $C_{load}$ is the lumped capacitance (including gate, drain diffusion, and interconnect parasitics), $V_{DD}$ is the supply voltage, and $f$ is operating frequency. Minimizing total transistor count directly reduces $C_{load}$, yielding proportional power savings.

\noindent\textbf{2. Short-Circuit Crowbar Power ($P_{sc}$):}\\
During switching transients, when the input waveform passes between $V_{thn}$ and $V_{DD} - |V_{thp}|$, both pull-up and pull-down networks conduct simultaneously, creating a transient low-impedance path between $V_{DD}$ and ground:
\begin{equation}
P_{sc} = \frac{\beta}{12} (V_{DD} - 2V_{th})^3 \frac{t_r + t_f}{T}
\label{eq:psc_form}
\end{equation}
where $t_r$ and $t_f$ are the input rise and fall times, and $T = 1/f$ is the clock period.

\noindent\textbf{3. Static Leakage Power ($P_{stat}$):}\\
Static power dissipation occurs in the absence of switching activity:
\begin{equation}
P_{stat} = I_{leakage} \cdot V_{DD}
\end{equation}
In deep-submicron regimes, total leakage current $I_{leakage}$ is governed by several distinct physical mechanisms [12]:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Subthreshold Leakage ($I_{sub}$):} Diffusion current flowing when $V_{gs} < V_{th}$, modeled as:
\begin{equation}
I_{sub} = \mu_0 C_{ox} \frac{W}{L} (m-1) v_T^2 \exp\left(\frac{V_{gs} - V_{th}}{m v_T}\right) \left[1 - \exp\left(-\frac{V_{ds}}{v_T}\right)\right]
\label{eq:isub_form}
\end{equation}
where $v_T = kT/q \approx 26\text{mV}$ at $300\text{K}$, and $m = 1 + C_d/C_{ox}$ is the subthreshold swing parameter.
\item \textbf{Drain-Induced Barrier Lowering (DIBL):} High drain potentials lower the source-channel electrostatic barrier in short channels, shifting the threshold voltage downward:
\begin{equation}
\Delta V_{th} = -\eta V_{ds}
\end{equation}
where $\eta$ is the DIBL coefficient. This barrier reduction exponentially accelerates subthreshold conduction.
\item \textbf{Gate-Induced Drain Leakage (GIDL):} Intense electric fields across the gate-drain overlap region trigger band-to-band carrier tunneling in the silicon surface depletion layer.
\item \textbf{Gate Dielectric Direct Tunneling ($I_{gate}$):} Thin gate dielectric layers ($t_{ox} < 2\text{nm}$) permit quantum-mechanical direct tunneling of electrons from the gate electrode into the channel.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Critical Evaluation of Digital Logic Design Paradigms}

Selecting an appropriate digital logic style involves balancing competing engineering requirements, including transistor count, propagation delay, parasitic capacitance, output swing, and noise margins.

\subsection{Conventional Static CMOS Logic (C-CMOS)}
Static CMOS implements logic functions using complementary PMOS pull-up networks (PUN) connected to $V_{DD}$ and NMOS pull-down networks (PDN) connected to GND.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Advantages:} Provides full rail-to-rail output swing ($0\text{V}$ to $V_{DD}$), strong noise margins, negligible static power in quiescent states, and broad compatibility with automated EDA standard-cell synthesis flows [7].
\item \textbf{Limitations:} For an $N$-input gate, $2N$ transistors are required. Implementing a complete 1-bit magnitude comparator requires 32 transistors, resulting in large parasitic junction capacitances, higher dynamic switching power, and significant silicon area overhead [1].
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Pass Transistor Logic (PTL)}
Pass Transistor Logic uses unipolar NMOS transistors as series switching elements to route input signals directly to output nodes.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Advantages:} Achieves a low transistor count (10 transistors for a 1-bit comparator), reducing input capacitance and dynamic power [6].
\item \textbf{Limitations:} Suffers from threshold voltage degradation: when passing a logic high ($V_{DD}$), the output rises only to $V_{DD} - V_{thn}$. The source-to-substrate voltage ($V_{sb} > 0$) exacerbates this drop via the body effect (Equation~\ref{eq:bodyeffect_ch2}), degrading noise margins and inducing static leakage in subsequent CMOS stages [12].
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Transmission Gate Logic (TGL)}
Transmission Gate Logic places an NMOS and a PMOS transistor in parallel to create a bidirectional transmission switch controlled by complementary signals ($C$ and $\overline{C}$).
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Advantages:} Eliminates threshold degradation by passing logic low through the NMOS and logic high through the PMOS, preserving full rail-to-rail voltage swings with good noise margins [7].
\item \textbf{Limitations:} Requires complementary control signals, necessitating additional inverter stages that increase transistor count (16 to 24 transistors) and routing complexity, while contributing to higher dynamic power dissipation [3].
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Gate Diffusion Input (GDI) Technique}

Introduced by Morgenshtein, Fish, and Wagner [5], the Gate Diffusion Input (GDI) technique is an effective low-power circuit design paradigm that permits the realization of complex logic functions with a substantially reduced transistor count. A basic GDI cell consists of a single PMOS and NMOS transistor pair with three independently driven input terminals, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:gdi_cell}:
\begin{itemize}
\item $G$: Common gate terminal connecting PMOS and NMOS gates.
\item $P$: Source/drain terminal of the PMOS transistor.
\item $N$: Source/drain terminal of the NMOS transistor.
\item $Out$: Common drain node forming the logic output.
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{Advantages of the GDI Technique:}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Substantial Transistor Count Reduction:} Complex functions (2:1 MUX, AND, OR) are realized using 2 transistors rather than 6 to 12 in static CMOS.
\item \textbf{Ultra-Low Power Dissipation:} Minimizing device count directly curtails internal node capacitances, lowering dynamic power and quiescent leakage.
\item \textbf{Compact Silicon Layout:} Smaller transistor counts reduce total cell area and parasitic interconnect length [5].
\end{enumerate}

\noindent\textbf{Design Considerations in GDI Circuits:}
In standard bulk CMOS processes with shared $N$-wells and $P$-substrates, floating diffusion inputs create non-zero source-to-substrate voltages ($V_{sb} > 0$), inducing body-effect threshold shifts. However, in 180nm bulk CMOS, these shifts remain modest, and the resulting signal levels remain sufficient for downstream digital switching without requiring complex level restoration.

\section{Comprehensive Critical Review of Prior Art}

To establish the state of the art in low-power digital comparator design, four published benchmark studies were analyzed in detail.

\subsection{Work 1: Kumar and Kumar (2017) -- Transistor Stacking Technique}
\textbf{Citation:} A. Kumar and M. Kumar, ``Improved Design of CMOS 1-Bit Comparator with Stacking Technique,'' \textit{International Journal of Current Engineering and Technology}, Vol. 7, no. 3, pp. 1044--1047, 2017 [1].

\noindent\textbf{Technical Approach:} This study investigated subthreshold leakage suppression in a 1-bit static CMOS comparator using the forced transistor stacking technique. Each original transistor of width $W$ was split into two series-connected transistors of width $W/2$. When both series devices are OFF, the intermediate node charges slightly above ground, generating a reverse body-to-source bias ($V_{bs} < 0$) and a negative gate-to-source bias ($V_{gs} < 0$). This self-reverse bias exponentially suppresses subthreshold leakage according to Equation~\ref{eq:isub_form}.

\noindent\textbf{Performance Metrics Reported:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Technology Node: TSMC 180nm CMOS ($V_{DD} = 1.8\text{V}$).
\item Propagation Delay: $49.38\text{ ns}$.
\item Average Power Dissipation: $174.352\text{ pW}$.
\item Power-Delay Product (PDP): $0.0086\text{ fJ}$ ($8.6\times 10^{-18}\text{ J}$).
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{Critical Analysis and Deficiencies:} Although the stacking technique effectively curtails subthreshold leakage current, the resulting propagation delay of $49.38\text{ ns}$ is severely degraded, making the circuit unsuitable for high-speed arithmetic datapaths. Furthermore, duplicating every transistor increases device count to over 45 transistors, substantially increasing silicon layout area and interconnect routing complexity [1].

\subsection{Work 2: Tailor, Sharma, and Kumar (2019) -- MTCMOS and Forced Stacking}
\textbf{Citation:} D. Tailor, T. Sharma, and M. Kumar, ``Design of CMOS Based 1-bit Comparator with MTCMOS and Forced Stack Technique in 180$\mu$m Technology,'' in \textit{Proc. IEEE International Conference on Signal Processing and Communication (ICSC)}, pp. 330--334, 2019 [2].

\noindent\textbf{Technical Approach:} Tailor et al. proposed a hybrid leakage reduction strategy combining Multi-Threshold CMOS (MTCMOS) with forced transistor stacking. High-$V_{th}$ sleep transistors were placed in series between the logic core and the power rails to choke off quiescent leakage in standby mode, while low-$V_{th}$ devices executed internal logic transitions during active operation.

\noindent\textbf{Performance Metrics Reported:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Technology Node: 180nm CMOS ($V_{DD} = 1.8\text{V}$).
\item Propagation Delay: $330.61\text{ ps}$.
\item Average Power Dissipation: $48.12\text{ pW}$.
\item Power-Delay Product (PDP): $0.0000159\text{ fJ}$ ($1.59\times 10^{-20}\text{ J}$).
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{Critical Analysis and Deficiencies:} The MTCMOS approach achieves competitive propagation delay and low leakage power. However, fabrication of dual-threshold devices requires additional photolithographic mask steps, increasing manufacturing costs. The architecture also introduces sleep-signal generation overhead and incurs wake-up latency penalties when transitioning between sleep and active modes [2].

\subsection{Work 3: Lubaba, Islam, Faisal, and Hasan (2020) -- Hybrid PTL-TG-CMOS Comparator}
\textbf{Citation:} S. Lubaba, M. S. Islam, K. M. Faisal, and M. Hasan, ``Design of a Two-Bit Magnitude Comparator Based on Pass Transistor, Transmission Gate and Conventional Static CMOS Logic,'' in \textit{Proc. IEEE Region 10 Symposium (TENSYMP)}, Dhaka, Bangladesh, pp. 1114--1117, 2020 [3].

\noindent\textbf{Technical Approach:} Lubaba et al. proposed a partitioned hybrid logic methodology for a 2-bit magnitude comparator in 90nm CMOS technology. Pass Transistor Logic was deployed for initial product terms, Transmission Gates implemented multiplexing and equivalence functions, and static CMOS inverters provided output signal buffering.

\noindent\textbf{Performance Metrics Reported:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Technology Node: Cadence Virtuoso 90nm CMOS ($V_{DD} = 1.0\text{V}$).
\item Propagation Delay: $0.192\text{ ns}$ ($192\text{ ps}$).
\item Average Power Dissipation: $7.792\text{ }\mu\text{W}$.
\item Power-Delay Product (PDP): $1.496\text{ fJ}$.
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{Critical Analysis and Deficiencies:} The hybrid design achieves sub-nanosecond switching latency, but its active power dissipation remains in the microwatt regime ($7.792\text{ }\mu\text{W}$), which is orders of magnitude higher than picowatt-level targets required for autonomous energy-harvesting and implantable medical applications [3]. Furthermore, the large transistor count required for multi-stage hybrid driving complicates layout automation and increases parasitic loading.

\subsection{Work 4: Sharma, Premananda, Raj, and Pandey (2025) -- Hybrid PTL-GDI-TGL Comparator}
\textbf{Citation:} Y. Sharma, B. S. Premananda, D. Raj, and K. Pandey, ``Design and Analysis of a 1-bit Comparator for Low Power VLSI Systems,'' in \textit{Proc. IEEE Third International Conference on Networks, Multimedia and Information Technology (NMITCON)}, Bengaluru, India, pp. 1--6, 2025 [4].

\noindent\textbf{Technical Approach:} Sharma et al. introduced a 1-bit comparator combining Transmission Gate Logic, Gate Diffusion Input, and Pass Transistor Logic. The architecture employs a 6T multiplexer-based full-adder module alongside TGL AND gates and GDI structures to balance delay and power.

\noindent\textbf{Performance Metrics Reported:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Technology Node: Cadence Spectre GPDK 90nm CMOS.
\item Propagation Delay: $5.5\text{ ps}$.
\item Average Power Dissipation: $7.300\text{ }\mu\text{W}$.
\item Power-Delay Product (PDP): $0.0400\text{ fJ}$.
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{Critical Analysis and Deficiencies:} Although the circuit achieves a low propagation delay of $5.5\text{ ps}$, the hybrid architecture requires 24 transistors and dissipates $7.3\text{ }\mu\text{W}$ of active power. The presence of three distinct logic families within a single cell complicates transistor sizing, increases sensitivity to process variations across corners, and introduces design complexity that limits multi-bit scalability [4].

\subsection{Foundational and Extended Works on GDI Logic}
Morgenshtein et al. originally established the GDI paradigm in 2002 [5], demonstrating power and area advantages across combinational cells. In subsequent research (2014) [14], Morgenshtein, Yuzhaninov, Kovshilovsky, and Fish investigated full-swing GDI topologies (FS-GDI) incorporating swing-restoration devices. While FS-GDI addresses voltage drops in deep-submicron regimes, the restoration transistors increase overall device count. Consequently, evaluating pure minimal-transistor GDI topologies operating in standard bulk CMOS remains a relevant design approach for ultra-low-power applications.

\section{Research Gap Analysis}

A systematic analysis of the published literature reveals four distinct research gaps:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Device Count Overhead in Leakage Suppression Techniques:}\\
Established leakage reduction schemes, such as forced transistor stacking [1] and MTCMOS [2], significantly increase transistor count (exceeding 45 transistors) and require dual-threshold fabrication steps that add process complexity and manufacturing cost.
\item \textbf{Elevated Active Power in Multi-Style Hybrids:}\\
While hybrid topologies [3, 4] achieve sub-nanosecond switching speeds, their power consumption remains in the microwatt regime ($7.3\text{ }\mu\text{W}$ to $7.79\text{ }\mu\text{W}$) with transistor counts of 24 or more. This power consumption is several orders of magnitude too high for battery-less, energy-harvesting, or implantable medical applications.
\item \textbf{Lack of Pure Minimal-Transistor GDI Benchmarks:}\\
Prior works often combine GDI with transmission gates or pass transistors, which increases device count. Rigorous investigations of a pure, minimal 10-transistor GDI comparator operating in the picowatt power regime with sub-nanosecond latency remain limited in published literature.
\item \textbf{Absence of Consistent Cross-Node Scaling Studies:}\\
Most published works evaluate designs at a single technology node. Comprehensive scaling studies tracing comparator performance from 180nm down to 45nm using consistent predictive device decks and simulation frameworks are scarce.
\end{enumerate}

This thesis addresses these research gaps through the design, implementation, and multi-node benchmarking of a 10-transistor pure GDI 1-bit magnitude comparator in 180nm CMOS technology, achieving picowatt-level power consumption, sub-nanosecond delay, and low Power-Delay Product.

\section{Comparator Architecture and Boolean Derivation}

The 1-bit comparator evaluates two single-bit inputs $A$ and $B$, generating three output signals ($A>B$, $A=B$, and $A<B$). The governing equations derived from the truth table (Table~\ref{tab:lit_truthtable}) are:

\begin{align}
(A > B) &= A \cdot \overline{B} \label{eq:agb_m} \\
(A = B) &= A \odot B = \overline{A \oplus B} = A B + \overline{A}\,\overline{B} \label{eq:aeb_m} \\
(A < B) &= \overline{A} \cdot B \label{eq:alb_m}
\end{align}

\subsection{Mapping Boolean Functions to GDI Cells}

The GDI technique allows the realization of these functions with minimal transistor count:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Greater-Than Output ($A>B = A \cdot \overline{B}$):}
Implemented using a 2-transistor GDI cell configured as Function F1:
\begin{itemize}
\item Gate input ($G$) is connected to signal $B$.
\item PMOS source ($P$) is connected to signal $A$.
\item NMOS source ($N$) is connected to Ground ($0\text{V}$).
\end{itemize}
When $B=0$, the PMOS conducts and passes the value of $A$. If $A=1$, the output is pulled to logic high ($1$). When $B=1$, the NMOS conducts and discharges the output to ground ($0\text{V}$). Thus, the output evaluates to $A \cdot \overline{B}$ using only 2 transistors.

\item \textbf{Less-Than Output ($A<B = \overline{A} \cdot B$):}
Implemented using a 2-transistor GDI cell in a complementary configuration:
\begin{itemize}
\item Gate input ($G$) is connected to signal $A$.
\item PMOS source ($P$) is connected to signal $B$.
\item NMOS source ($N$) is connected to Ground ($0\text{V}$).
\end{itemize}
When $A=0$, the PMOS conducts and passes $B$. If $B=1$, the output becomes $1$. When $A=1$, the NMOS discharges the output to ground ($0\text{V}$), producing $\overline{A} \cdot B$ with 2 transistors.

\item \textbf{Equality Output ($A=B = A \odot B$):}
The XNOR function is synthesized using a GDI multiplexer structure:
\begin{itemize}
\item Gate input ($G$) is connected to signal $A$.
\item PMOS source ($P$) is connected to the inverted input $\overline{B}$.
\item NMOS source ($N$) is connected to input $B$.
\end{itemize}
When $A=0$, the PMOS passes $\overline{B}$. If $B=0$, the output is $\overline{0}=1$; if $B=1$, the output is $\overline{1}=0$. When $A=1$, the NMOS passes $B$. If $B=1$, the output is $1$; if $B=0$, the output is $0$. This realizes the complete XNOR equivalence operation.

\item \textbf{Input Inversion Stages:}
Standard 2-transistor inverters generate the inverted signals $\overline{A}$ and $\overline{B}$ required by the core logic cells.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Transistor Sizing and Channel Geometry}

Because the hole mobility ($\mu_p \approx 200\text{ cm}^2/\text{V}\cdot\text{s}$) in silicon is roughly two to three times lower than the electron mobility ($\mu_n \approx 500\text{ cm}^2/\text{V}\cdot\text{s}$), PMOS transistors are sized wider than NMOS transistors to achieve balanced pull-up and pull-down drive capabilities:

\begin{equation}
\beta_n = \mu_n C_{ox} \left(\frac{W}{L}\right)_n, \quad \beta_p = \mu_p C_{ox} \left(\frac{W}{L}\right)_p
\end{equation}

For balanced switching ($t_{pHL} \approx t_{pLH}$), the sizing ratio is chosen as:
\begin{equation}
\frac{W_p}{W_n} \approx \frac{\mu_n}{\mu_p} \approx 2.0 \text{ to } 2.5
\end{equation}

\section{Simulation Testbench and Measurement Methodology}

The LTSpice testbench was configured to evaluate functional correctness, propagation delay, and power dissipation.

\subsection{Input Signal Configuration}

The input signals were designed to exercise all four binary input combinations within the simulation window:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Input A:} A periodic pulse waveform with a period of 20 ns and pulse width of 10 ns, generating a 50 MHz square wave toggling between 0V and 1.8V.
\item \textbf{Input B:} A periodic pulse waveform with a period of 80 ns and pulse width of 40 ns, generating a 12.5 MHz square wave toggling between 0V and 1.8V.
\end{itemize}

The combination of these two input frequencies produces the following input vector sequence within each 80 ns cycle:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $(A=0, B=0)$: $0 \le t < 10\text{ ns}$
\item $(A=1, B=0)$: $10 \le t < 20\text{ ns}$
\item $(A=0, B=1)$: $40 \le t < 50\text{ ns}$
\item $(A=1, B=1)$: $50 \le t < 60\text{ ns}$
\end{enumerate}

This sequence ensures that all four possible input combinations are applied during the transient simulation, enabling complete functional verification.

\subsection{Propagation Delay Measurement}
Propagation delay is measured between the 50\% voltage point of the switching input and the 50\% point of the resulting output transition:
\begin{equation}
t_{pHL} = t_{out(50\% \text{ fall})} - t_{in(50\% \text{ rise})}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
t_{pLH} = t_{out(50\% \text{ rise})} - t_{in(50\% \text{ fall})}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
t_{pd} = \frac{t_{pHL} + t_{pLH}}{2}
\label{eq:tpd_def}
\end{equation}
Rise time ($t_r$) and fall time ($t_f$) are extracted between the 10\% and 90\% points of the output voltage transitions.

\subsection{Average Power and PDP Computation}
Average power dissipation ($P_{avg}$) is computed by integrating the instantaneous supply current $I(V_{DD}, t)$ over the complete simulation duration $T_{sim} = 100\text{ ns}$:
\begin{equation}
P_{avg} = \frac{1}{T_{sim}} \int_0^{T_{sim}} V_{DD} \cdot I(V_{DD}, t) \, dt
\label{eq:pavg_integral}
\end{equation}
The Power-Delay Product (PDP), which quantifies the energy efficiency of the circuit per switching event, is calculated as:
\begin{equation}
\text{PDP} = P_{avg} \times t_{pd}
\label{eq:pdp_def}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Simulation Accuracy and Convergence Settings}

To ensure accurate transient simulation results, the following LTSpice simulation control parameters were configured:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Maximum time step:} 0.05 ns (50 ps), providing at least 20 simulation points per input rise/fall transition.
\item \textbf{Start time:} 0 ns, with the transient window extending to 100 ns.
\item \textbf{Integration method:} Modified trapezoidal (LTSpice default), ensuring stable convergence for circuits with sharp switching transients.
\item \textbf{Tolerance settings:} Default relative tolerance ($reltol = 0.001$) and absolute tolerance ($abstol = 1\text{pA}$, $vntol = 1\mu\text{V}$) for high-precision current and voltage resolution.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Power Dissipation Analysis}

The superior power performance of the GDI comparator can be attributed to several factors:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Reduced Capacitive Loading:} With only 10 transistors, the total gate, drain, and interconnect capacitance is minimized, directly reducing dynamic switching power ($P_{dyn} \propto C_{load}$).
\item \textbf{Minimal Internal Nodes:} The GDI design has fewer internal switching nodes compared to static CMOS, reducing the total capacitance that must be charged and discharged during each logic transition.
\item \textbf{Efficient Signal Routing:} GDI cells route input signals directly through the transistor diffusion terminals, avoiding the need for separate buffering stages that consume additional power.
\item \textbf{Low Short-Circuit Current:} The complementary nature of the GDI cell (one PMOS, one NMOS) limits the simultaneous conduction window during input transitions, reducing short-circuit power dissipation.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Propagation Delay Analysis}

The GDI comparator achieves the lowest propagation delay among all evaluated designs due to:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Direct Signal Path:} GDI cells provide a direct signal path from input to output through only one transistor (either PMOS or NMOS), minimizing the RC delay.
\item \textbf{No Threshold Voltage Drop:} Unlike PTL, the GDI cell configuration ensures that both logic-high and logic-low levels are transmitted without significant voltage degradation, enabling faster switching in downstream gates.
\item \textbf{Reduced Fan-In:} Each GDI output cell has a fan-in of only 2 (from the gate terminal and one diffusion terminal), keeping input capacitance low.
\item \textbf{Balanced Drive Strength:} The PMOS/NMOS sizing ratio of 2:1 provides reasonably balanced pull-up and pull-down switching transitions.
\end{enumerate}

In modern engineering education and professional practice under the Washington Accord of the International Engineering Alliance (IEA) and the Board of Accreditation for Engineering and Technical Education (BAETE), capstone engineering projects and research theses must address Complex Engineering Problems (P1--P7 / WP1--WP7) and demonstrate Complex Engineering Activities (A1--A5 / EA1--EA5). A problem is classified as complex if it cannot be resolved through routine engineering practice, standard handbook formulas, or catalog component selection, but instead demands research-based knowledge, critical trade-off navigation, advanced modeling, and original synthesis.

The transistor-level design, optimization, and characterization of the 10-transistor Gate Diffusion Input (GDI) 1-bit magnitude comparator presented in this thesis fully satisfies every criterion defined by the Washington Accord. The following subsections present the comprehensive, evidence-based justification for each attribute.

\subsection{Detailed Justification of Complex Engineering Problem Attributes (P1--P7)}

\subsubsection{P1: Depth of Knowledge Required}
\textbf{Criterion:} \textit{Cannot be resolved without in-depth engineering knowledge at the level of one or more of the Knowledge Profiles (WK1 to WK8), which includes advanced fundamental principles, research literature, and specialized engineering science.}

\noindent\textbf{Justification and Evidence in This Thesis:}
The design of an ultra-low-power digital comparator operating in the picowatt power regime cannot be accomplished using introductory digital logic or truth-table textbook rules. It requires in-depth mastery across multiple advanced domains of electrical and electronic engineering science:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Solid-State Semiconductor Physics:} Accurately modeling carrier transport mechanisms in deep-submicron MOSFETs under non-equilibrium conditions, including channel carrier velocity saturation, mobility degradation due to transverse electric fields ($\mu_{eff}$), and 2D electrostatic drain-field coupling.
\item \textbf{Nanoscale Leakage Mechanisms:} Formulating subthreshold conduction using Boltzmann thermodynamic energy distribution models (Equation~\ref{eq:isub_form}), where subthreshold current $I_{sub}$ scales exponentially with gate-to-source bias $V_{gs}$ and thermal voltage $v_T = kT/q$. Addressing the physical limit of subthreshold swing ($SS \ge 60\text{ mV/dec}$) at room temperature is essential for leakage suppression.
\item \textbf{Body-Effect Mechanics in Floating-Source Topologies:} In standard static CMOS, transistor sources are tied directly to stable DC rails ($V_{DD}$ and GND), keeping source-to-substrate voltage at zero ($V_{sb} = 0$). In contrast, GDI applies dynamic inputs directly to PMOS and NMOS diffusion terminals ($P$ and $N$). When the source voltage floats above substrate ground ($V_{sb} > 0$), the depletion layer widens, causing dynamic threshold voltage modulation:
\begin{equation}
\Delta V_{th} = \gamma \left(\sqrt{2\phi_F + V_{sb}} - \sqrt{2\phi_F}\right)
\end{equation}
Predicting and managing this shift to ensure valid logic transitions across all four input vectors requires graduate-level semiconductor device theory (Chapters 2 and 3).
\item \textbf{Advanced BSIM3v3 Compact Device Modeling:} Applying the Berkeley BSIM3v3 Level 49 model deck within LTSpice, which includes over 40 physical device parameters governing threshold shifts, DIBL coefficients ($\eta$), parasitic junction overlap capacitances, and non-linear bulk charge effects.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{P2: Range of Conflicting Requirements}
\textbf{Criterion:} \textit{Involve wide-ranging or conflicting technical, engineering, and other issues.}

\noindent\textbf{Justification and Evidence in This Thesis:}
The development of the proposed comparator required resolving several deeply coupled, conflicting engineering trade-offs:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Transistor Count Minimization vs. Signal Driving Integrity:} Slicing transistor count from 32 down to 10 (a 68.75\% reduction) substantially decreases silicon area and parasitic capacitive loading. However, eliminating intermediate buffering stages means output nodes are driven directly through pass-diffusion channels, creating a trade-off between device count and drive strength that required careful transistor sizing optimization.
\item \textbf{Ultra-Low Power Dissipation vs. Propagation Delay:} Lowering dynamic power dissipation ($P_{dyn} \propto C_{load} V_{DD}^2 f$) demands minimizing transistor aspect ratios ($W/L$) to reduce capacitive loading. Conversely, minimizing propagation delay ($t_{pd}$) requires larger drive currents ($I_{ds} \propto W/L$), which increases device width and parasitic capacitance. The proposed design successfully balances this trade-off, achieving an average power of 34.18~pW while maintaining a sub-nanosecond propagation delay of 336.00~ps.
\item \textbf{Switching Symmetry vs. Input Capacitive Load:} Due to the fundamental carrier mobility disparity between electrons and holes in silicon ($\mu_n \approx 2.5\mu_p$), PMOS pull-up devices must be sized wider than NMOS devices ($W_p/W_n \approx 2.0$) to balance high-to-low and low-to-high transition times. However, widening PMOS gates increases input gate capacitance ($C_{gg}$), which increases dynamic power and degrades upstream driver speed.
\item \textbf{Process Cost vs. Energy Efficiency:} While previously reported MTCMOS techniques [2] achieve low leakage by introducing high-$V_{th}$ sleep transistors, they require additional fabrication masks that increase manufacturing costs. The proposed design achieves superior energy efficiency (PDP of 0.0000114~fJ vs. 0.0000159~fJ in MTCMOS) within a standard single-threshold bulk CMOS process.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{P3: Depth of Analysis Required}
\textbf{Criterion:} \textit{Have no obvious solution and require abstract thinking and originality in analysis to formulate suitable models.}

\noindent\textbf{Justification and Evidence in This Thesis:}
Standard digital design relies on pre-characterized standard cell libraries where timing and power are retrieved from lookup tables. For non-conventional GDI logic, no commercial automated cell library exists. Establishing the validity, timing, and energy efficiency of the proposed circuit required extensive analytical modeling and numerical SPICE simulations:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Time-Domain Integral Power Formulation:} Instantaneous supply current $I(V_{DD}, t)$ exhibits sharp sub-nanosecond switching spikes superimposed on sub-picoampere static leakage. Accurately determining true average power required continuous numerical integration over the full 100ns transient window:
\begin{equation}
P_{avg} = \frac{1}{T_{sim}} \int_0^{T_{sim}} V_{DD} \cdot I(V_{DD}, t) \, dt
\end{equation}
\item \textbf{Transient Slew Rate and Threshold Point Extraction:} Propagation delay cannot be estimated from simple RC delay models because the non-linear on-resistance of the GDI pass network varies dynamically with node voltages. High-precision .meas SPICE directives were configured to extract delays at precise 50\% voltage crossing thresholds ($0.9\text{V}$ in a $1.8\text{V}$ domain) across all four switching transitions.
\item \textbf{Cross-Node Predictive Scaling Analysis:} Abstract analytical modeling was conducted across three technology nodes (180nm, 90nm, and 45nm PTM), evaluating how voltage scaling ($1.8\text{V} \rightarrow 1.2\text{V} \rightarrow 1.0\text{V}$) and dimensional scaling affect delay stability and leakage current density.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{P4: Familiarity of Issues}
\textbf{Criterion:} \textit{Involve infrequently encountered issues or non-standard engineering problems.}

\noindent\textbf{Justification and Evidence in This Thesis:}
In routine digital CMOS design, all transistor sources and drains are connected to fixed DC rails ($V_{DD}$ or GND) or to internal nodes of complementary pull-up/pull-down networks, an architecture supported by automated synthesis tools (e.g., Synopsys Design Compiler). 

In sharp contrast, the Gate Diffusion Input (GDI) technique applies dynamic, varying logic signals directly to the PMOS and NMOS source/drain diffusion terminals ($P$ and $N$). This introduces several non-standard design issues:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Dynamic Floating Bulk Biasing:} Because the PMOS bulk is tied to $V_{DD}$ and the NMOS bulk is tied to GND in standard single-well bulk CMOS, applying signals to diffusion terminals causes the source-to-body potential ($V_{sb}$) to vary dynamically with the input vector. This dynamic modulation alters threshold voltages in real time during circuit operation.
\item \textbf{Bidirectional Conduction Paths:} Unlike static CMOS where current flows unidirectionally from $V_{DD}$ to output or from output to GND, GDI cells permit bidirectional charge transfer between diffusion inputs and output nodes depending on input polarities.
\item \textbf{Absence of EDA Synthesis Support:} Standard digital design flows cannot automatically synthesize, place, or route GDI logic cells. The designer must perform custom, transistor-level schematic entry, manual aspect ratio sizing, and analog-level SPICE characterization.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{P6: Extent of Stakeholder Involvement and Diverse Needs}
\textbf{Criterion:} \textit{Involve diverse groups of stakeholders with widely varying and conflicting needs.}

\noindent\textbf{Justification and Evidence in This Thesis:}
The optimization of digital comparator primitives impacts several distinct stakeholder groups across the semiconductor and systems ecosystem, each presenting competing operational demands:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Biomedical Engineers and Patients:} For implantable medical electronics (cardiac pacemakers, cochlear implants, neural recording interfaces), battery longevity is paramount. Minimizing comparator power to the picowatt regime directly extends implant operating lifetime, reducing the frequency of surgical battery replacement procedures and improving patient safety.
\item \textbf{IoT and Edge Infrastructure Developers:} Autonomous sensor nodes deployed in smart agriculture, structural monitoring, and industrial IoT require energy-harvesting operation without battery replacement. These systems require digital processing elements that consume minimal quiescent power.
\item \textbf{Microprocessor and Computing Architects:} In high-performance microprocessors and high-speed Flash ADCs, comparators operate on critical timing paths (e.g., L1 cache tag-matching). These stakeholders prioritize sub-nanosecond propagation delay and high throughput, while seeking to prevent localized thermal hotspots.
\item \textbf{Semiconductor Foundries and Manufacturing Engineers:} Foundries require high fabrication yield, minimal silicon area consumption, and compatibility with standard, cost-effective single-well bulk CMOS manufacturing without expensive specialized implant masks.
\end{enumerate}
The proposed 10-transistor GDI comparator successfully addresses these diverse stakeholder needs by delivering 34.18~pW power dissipation, 336~ps delay, 68.75\% area reduction, and full compatibility with standard bulk CMOS technology.

\subsubsection{P7: Interdependence of Engineering Sub-Problems}
\textbf{Criterion:} \textit{Are high-level problems that include many sub-problems or parts that are strongly interdependent.}

\noindent\textbf{Justification and Evidence in This Thesis:}
In deep-submicron GDI circuit design, no individual parameter can be tuned in isolation. Modifying any single circuit attribute triggers a cascade of coupled effects across the entire design space:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Device Width Coupling:} Increasing PMOS channel width ($W_p$) to reduce rise time ($t_r$) increases transconductance $\beta_p$, but simultaneously expands gate area ($W \times L$), which increases gate oxide capacitance ($C_{ox} W L$) and drain junction capacitance ($C_{db}$). This additional capacitance increases dynamic power dissipation ($C_{load} V_{DD}^2 f$) and increases the capacitive load presented to upstream driving stages, potentially degrading overall system delay.
\item \textbf{Supply Voltage Interdependence:} Scaling supply voltage ($V_{DD}$) from $1.8\text{V}$ down to $1.0\text{V}$ yields quadratic savings in dynamic power, but reduces gate overdrive $(V_{DD} - V_{th})$, leading to increased propagation delay and heightened sensitivity to threshold voltage variations.
\item \textbf{Topological Interdependence:} The equality output ($A=B$) relies on an XNOR GDI multiplexer that receives inverted input $\overline{B}$ generated by the input inverter stage ($M_3, M_4$). Consequently, the delay and output slew rate of the inverter directly influence the switching delay and short-circuit power of the downstream equality stage.
\end{itemize}
These coupled interactions require a holistic circuit-level optimization approach rather than decoupled, stage-by-stage design.

\chapter{CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK}

\section{Summary of Research}

This thesis has presented a comprehensive investigation into the design, implementation, and performance characterization of low-power 1-bit magnitude comparator circuits using the Gate Diffusion Input (GDI) technique. The research encompassed theoretical analysis, circuit synthesis, transistor-level simulation, and multi-parameter benchmarking against three established digital logic styles: conventional static CMOS, Pass Transistor Logic (PTL), and Transmission Gate Logic (TGL).

The following key activities were completed during this research:
\begin{enumerate}
\item An extensive literature review covering MOSFET physics, power dissipation mechanisms, digital logic design paradigms, and published comparator architectures.
\item Mathematical derivation and Karnaugh-map minimization of Boolean functions for all three comparator outputs ($A>B$, $A=B$, $A<B$).
\item Systematic mapping of Boolean functions to optimized GDI cell configurations, achieving a complete 1-bit comparator with only 10 transistors.
\item Transistor-level design and implementation of four comparator topologies in LTSpice using the 180nm Predictive Technology Model (PTM).
\item Comprehensive transient simulation and functional verification across all four input combinations.
\item Extraction and comparison of key performance metrics: rise time, fall time, propagation delay, average power dissipation, and Power-Delay Product (PDP).
\item Multi-node technology scaling evaluation across 180nm, 90nm, and 45nm PTM technology decks.
\item Comparative benchmarking against four published IEEE/academic papers.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusions}

The circuit was modeled and simulated in LTSpice using the 180nm Berkeley Predictive Technology Model (PTM) with a 1.8V supply voltage. The primary conclusions of this work are summarized as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Functional Correctness:} Transient simulation confirmed that the 10-transistor GDI comparator produces correct output voltage levels across all four binary input combinations ($(A,B) = 00, 01, 10, 11$).
\item \textbf{Power Minimization:} The proposed GDI design achieved an average power dissipation of 34.18~pW, representing a 73.83\% reduction compared to static CMOS (130.61~pW) and a 10.34\% reduction compared to PTL (38.12~pW).
\item \textbf{Propagation Delay Improvement:} For the equality ($A=B$) output, the GDI comparator achieved a propagation delay of 336.00~ps, outperforming static CMOS (398.09~ps) by 15.59\%, PTL (421.77~ps) by 20.33\%, and TGL (587.24~ps) by 42.78\%.
\item \textbf{Energy Efficiency:} The proposed GDI comparator achieved a Power-Delay Product (PDP) of 0.0000114~fJ, outperforming all benchmark designs evaluated in this study, including the MTCMOS design by Tailor et al. [2] (0.0000159 fJ).
\item \textbf{Technology Scalability:} Multi-node evaluations across 180nm, 90nm, and 45nm PTM decks confirmed consistent performance scaling, with the GDI design maintaining stable delay performance (less than 1\% variation) across all technology nodes.
\item \textbf{Literature Superiority:} The proposed design achieves the lowest PDP among all published comparator designs analyzed in this study, establishing a new benchmark for energy-efficient comparator design.
\end{enumerate}

These results demonstrate that the GDI technique provides an effective approach for designing energy-efficient digital comparators for low-power VLSI applications.

\section{Contributions of This Thesis}

The principal technical contributions of this thesis include:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Complete GDI Comparator Design:} A fully functional 10-transistor GDI-based 1-bit magnitude comparator generating all three comparison outputs ($A>B$, $A=B$, $A<B$) with minimal device count.
\item \textbf{Comprehensive Four-Way Benchmarking:} Systematic performance comparison of the GDI comparator against static CMOS (32T), PTL (10T), and TGL (16T) designs under identical simulation conditions.
\item \textbf{Multi-Node Scaling Study:} Cross-technology evaluation across 180nm, 90nm, and 45nm PTM nodes demonstrating consistent GDI performance scaling.
\item \textbf{Literature Benchmark Analysis:} Detailed critical analysis and quantitative comparison against four published comparator architectures.
\item \textbf{Design Methodology Documentation:} Complete documentation of the GDI cell mapping procedure, transistor sizing methodology, and simulation testbench configuration, providing a reproducible framework for future research.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitations of the Current Study}

The findings of this study should be interpreted in the context of the following limitations:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Threshold Voltage and Body Effect:} In standard bulk CMOS processes with common well ties, internal GDI nodes may experience body-effect-induced threshold voltage shifts, which can slightly affect output swing under specific load conditions.
\item \textbf{Schematic-Level Modeling:} Simulations were conducted at the schematic level using PTM device models with lumped parasitics. Physical layout routing parasitics and 3D interconnect effects were not included.
\item \textbf{Environmental Variations:} Simulations were performed at nominal operating conditions ($27^\circ\text{C}$, $V_{DD}=1.8\text{V}$). Full Process, Voltage, and Temperature (PVT) corner analysis and Monte Carlo statistical mismatch evaluations were not conducted.
\item \textbf{Fan-Out Loading:} The simulations did not include fan-out loading effects from subsequent logic stages, which would increase output capacitance and affect delay measurements.
\item \textbf{Process Technology Limitations:} In standard bulk CMOS processes, the body terminals of PMOS and NMOS transistors are connected to fixed potentials ($V_{DD}$ and GND, respectively), which may limit the full potential of GDI cells. Twin-well or SOI processes would allow independent body biasing, potentially improving GDI performance further.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Recommendations for Future Work}

The following directions are recommended for future research:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Multi-Bit Scaling:} Extend the 1-bit GDI comparator to multi-bit (4-bit, 8-bit, 32-bit) hierarchical and tree-based comparator architectures to evaluate the scalability of power and delay advantages in practical digital systems.
\item \textbf{Physical Layout and Post-Layout Extraction:} Create full custom physical layouts using Cadence Virtuoso, extract parasitic RC netlists, and perform post-layout simulations to evaluate silicon area and interconnect loading effects.
\item \textbf{Advanced Technology Nodes:} Implement and evaluate the GDI comparator in emerging technologies such as FinFET, Gate-All-Around (GAA) nanosheets, and Fully Depleted Silicon-on-Insulator (FDSOI).
\item \textbf{Statistical and Corner Verification:} Perform Monte Carlo mismatch simulations and PVT corner evaluations (Fast-Fast, Slow-Slow, Typical-Typical) across supply variations ($\pm 10\%$) and temperature extremes ($-40^\circ\text{C}$ to $+125^\circ\text{C}$).
\item \textbf{Subsystem Integration:} Integrate the GDI comparator into larger functional blocks, such as Flash ADCs, ALU data-path sorting networks, and priority encoders, to evaluate system-level energy savings.
\item \textbf{Dynamic Voltage and Frequency Scaling (DVFS):} Evaluate the GDI comparator's performance under variable supply voltage conditions to assess its suitability for DVFS-enabled systems.
\item \textbf{Radiation Hardening:} Investigate the single-event upset (SEU) tolerance of GDI circuits for space and radiation-environment applications.
\item \textbf{Machine Learning-Aided Optimization:} Apply genetic algorithms or machine learning techniques to optimize transistor sizing and GDI cell configurations for specific performance targets.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{B.1 Attribute P1: Depth of Knowledge Required}

\begin{enumerate}

\subsection*{B.2 Attribute P2: Range of Conflicting Requirements}
\textbf{Criterion Definition:} \textit{Involve wide-ranging or conflicting technical, engineering, and other issues.}

\noindent\textbf{Technical Evidence and Demonstration:}
The circuit optimization exists at the intersection of several conflicting performance dimensions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Transistor Count vs. Drive Strength:} Reducing transistor count by 68.75\% (from 32T in static CMOS to 10T in GDI) eliminates intermediate buffering stages, forcing outputs to be driven through pass-diffusion channels. Sizing must ensure adequate drive current without re-introducing parasitic capacitance.
\item \textbf{Power Minimization vs. Switching Latency:} Minimizing average power dissipation (achieving 34.18~pW) requires sub-micron device widths ($W_n = 360\text{nm}, W_p = 720\text{nm}$) to curtail load capacitance ($C_{load}$). However, reducing widths lowers saturation drive currents ($I_{ds,sat}$), creating a trade-off against propagation delay ($t_{pd} = 336\text{ps}$).
\item \textbf{Switching Symmetry vs. Input Load:} Hole mobility in silicon ($\mu_p \approx 200\text{ cm}^2/\text{V}\cdot\text{s}$) is less than half that of electron mobility ($\mu_n \approx 500\text{ cm}^2/\text{V}\cdot\text{s}$). Sizing PMOS devices wider ($W_p/W_n = 2.0$) restores pull-up drive strength and balances rise and fall times, but increases input capacitance, which increases dynamic switching power ($P_{dyn} \propto C_{in} V_{DD}^2 f$).
\item \textbf{Manufacturing Complexity vs. Energy Efficiency:} Achieving high energy efficiency without resorting to expensive multi-threshold fabrication processes (such as MTCMOS, which requires extra mask steps and introduces wake-up latency).
\end{enumerate}

\item Cross-node scaling modeling across three technology generations (180nm, 90nm, and 45nm PTM), analyzing how supply voltage scaling affects noise margins, gate overdrive, and leakage currents.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent\textbf{Mathematical Models of Leakage and GDI Conduction}

\vspace{0.5cm}

\noindent\textbf{D.1 Subthreshold Current Model}

\noindent The subthreshold drain current in a MOSFET operating below threshold is described by the following expression:
\begin{equation}
I_{sub} = \mu_0 C_{ox} \frac{W}{L} (m-1) v_T^2 \exp\left(\frac{V_{gs} - V_{th}}{m v_T}\right) \left[1 - \exp\left(-\frac{V_{ds}}{v_T}\right)\right]
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\mu_0$: Low-field carrier mobility ($\text{cm}^2/\text{V}\cdot\text{s}$)
\item $C_{ox}$: Gate oxide capacitance per unit area ($\text{F}/\text{cm}^2$)
\item $W/L$: Transistor aspect ratio
\item $m$: Subthreshold swing coefficient ($m = 1 + C_d/C_{ox}$, where $C_d$ is the depletion capacitance)
\item $v_T = kT/q$: Thermal voltage ($\approx 26\text{ mV}$ at $300\text{ K}$)
\item $V_{gs}$: Gate-to-source voltage
\item $V_{th}$: Threshold voltage
\item $V_{ds}$: Drain-to-source voltage
\end{itemize}

The subthreshold swing ($SS$) defines the gate voltage change required to change the drain current by one decade:
\begin{equation}
SS = m \cdot v_T \cdot \ln(10) = \frac{kT}{q} \cdot \ln(10) \cdot \left(1 + \frac{C_d}{C_{ox}}\right)
\end{equation}
At room temperature ($T = 300\text{ K}$), the ideal minimum subthreshold swing is:
\begin{equation}
SS_{min} = \frac{kT}{q} \cdot \ln(10) = 26\text{ mV} \times 2.303 \approx 60\text{ mV/decade}
\end{equation}

\vspace{0.5cm}
\noindent\textbf{D.2 GDI Cell Conduction Analysis}

\noindent For the basic GDI F1 function ($N=0$, $P=B$, $G=A$, implementing $\overline{A} \cdot B$):

\noindent\textit{Case 1: $A = 0$ (Gate Low)}\\
The PMOS transistor turns ON ($V_{gs,p} = 0 - V_{DD} = -V_{DD} < V_{thp}$), and the NMOS transistor turns OFF ($V_{gs,n} = 0 < V_{thn}$). The output is connected to the PMOS source ($P = B$), so $Out = B$.

\noindent\textit{Case 2: $A = 1$ (Gate High)}\\
The NMOS transistor turns ON ($V_{gs,n} = V_{DD} > V_{thn}$), and the PMOS transistor turns OFF ($V_{gs,p} = V_{DD} - V_{DD} = 0 > V_{thp}$). The output is connected to the NMOS source ($N = 0$), so $Out = 0$.

\noindent Therefore, the output function is:
\begin{equation}
Out = \begin{cases} B & \text{if } A = 0 \\ 0 & \text{if } A = 1 \end{cases} = \overline{A} \cdot B
\end{equation}

\vspace{0.5cm}
\noindent\textbf{D.3 GDI XNOR Cell for Equality Detection}

\noindent For the GDI multiplexer configuration ($N=B$, $P=\overline{B}$, $G=A$, implementing $A \odot B$):

\noindent\textit{Case 1: $A = 0$}\\
PMOS conducts, NMOS off. $Out = P = \overline{B}$.

\noindent\textit{Case 2: $A = 1$}\\
NMOS conducts, PMOS off. $Out = N = B$.

\noindent The complete truth table verification:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|}
\hline
$A$ & $B$ & $Out$ & $A \odot B$ \\
\hline
0 & 0 & $\overline{0} = 1$ & 1 \\
\hline
0 & 1 & $\overline{1} = 0$ & 0 \\
\hline
1 & 0 & $0$ & 0 \\
\hline
1 & 1 & $1$ & 1 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent This confirms that the GDI cell correctly implements the XNOR (equivalence) function for the $A=B$ output of the comparator.

\vspace{0.5cm}
\noindent\textbf{D.4 Power-Delay Product Derivation}

\noindent The Power-Delay Product (PDP) represents the energy consumed per switching event:
\begin{equation}
PDP = P_{avg} \times t_{pd}
\end{equation}

\noindent Substituting the dynamic power expression:
\begin{equation}
PDP = (\alpha \cdot C_L \cdot V_{DD}^2 \cdot f) \times t_{pd}
\end{equation}

\noindent Since $f = 1/(2 \cdot t_{pd})$ for a circuit switching at its maximum frequency:
\begin{equation}
PDP = \frac{\alpha \cdot C_L \cdot V_{DD}^2}{2}
\end{equation}

\noindent This shows that PDP depends primarily on the load capacitance $C_L$ and the square of the supply voltage $V_{DD}$, confirming the two main pathways for energy reduction: minimizing capacitance (fewer transistors, as achieved by GDI) and reducing supply voltage.
